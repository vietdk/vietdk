hich are supporting domestic demand and overall economic activity.
Vietnam’s external position remains stable, supported by steady trade and improving foreign exchange conditions, with reserves expected to recover after pressure from a stronger U.S. dolla
r.
Standard Chartered noted that Vietnam’s 2025 performance showed strong economic resilience, reflecting competitive manufacturing capacity and the ability of businesses to adapt to shifting global conditions.
The bank said monetary policy may remain su
pportive in the near term, though tighter global financial conditions and trade developments could add pressure to the exchange rate.

Vietnam’s Credit May Shift away from Property, Lower-Tier Banks Face Pressure
The State Bank of Vietnam (SBV), the country’s Central Bank, is steering 2026 credit growth toward about 15%, with lending expected to move away from real estate and into consum
er finance and major infrastructure, according to an assessment by Yuanta Vietnam.
Yuanta said a group of large lenders, including Vietcombank, VietinBank, MBBank and VPBank, could secure credit growth above 17%, supported by stronger balance sheets and r
egulatory assessments.
Banks with credit limits below 14% face what Yuanta described as “dual pressure,” combining limited capital buffers and higher risks of bad debt, particularly as lending to property developers remains tightly controlled.
As propert
y credit cools, consumer lending is expected to expand, with lenders such as VPBank, Techcombank and VIB likely to prioritize retail loans to fully utilize their assigned growth limits and support domestic consumption.
Meanwhile, state-owned banks are exp
ected to direct large credit flows into projects including Long Thanh International Airport and the North–South Expressway, benefiting construction and materials firms as authorities emphasize risk-controlled credit allocation.
The central bank has instru
cted lenders to manage not only credit size but also lending structure and timing in 2026, underscoring a shift toward tighter risk oversight amid uneven sector recovery. (

Digital Citizen Tax Incentives Could Add $2.2B/Year to Vietnam Budget: Ministry
Vietnam’s Ministry of Public Security said tax incentives for active digital c
itizens could generate more than VND56 trillion ($2.2 billion) annually, outweighing an estimated VND14.9 trillion revenue loss, under a revised policy proposal reviewed in early 2026.
The policy centers on VNeID as a unified digital ID and authentication
platform, combined with a scoring system that rewards citizens for using online public services and digital transactions, without linking scores to penalties or restrictions on legal rights.
Earlier proposals included tax and fee reductions of 10%–100% a
cross 127 items, including civil registration and vehicle procedures, prompting concerns from the Ministry of Finance over potential pressure on state budget revenues.
The Ministry of Public Security argued the short-term revenue loss should be treated as
development investment, estimating economic spillovers of VND22.4 trillion a year, based on a 1.5 multiplier, with VND3.5 trillion–4.5 trillion recoverable through indirect taxes.
With more than 80 million digital IDs issued by 2025, officials said encou
raging one-third of users to become active digital citizens could lift the digital economy to about $26 billion, while improving tax transparency for businesses and household traders.
The ministry estimated stronger e-commerce activity and reduced tax eva
sion could add nearly VND21 trillion annually, including about VND8 trillion from improved tax collection and VND13 trillion from expanded digital commerce.
To address fiscal concerns, the revised draft caps some tax reductions, limits personal income tax
cuts to VND1 million per transaction, and adjusts incentive scoring thresholds, aiming to balance digital adoption with budget stability. (
